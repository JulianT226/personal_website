Demon Copperhead

by Barbara Kingsolver
Mar 29, 2026

This goes very well with Empire of Pain; both books tell two sides of the same story. EoP will provide you with astonishing background on how the opioid epidemic came to be, but despite it being factual it doesn't really tell the truth. You need an incredible piece of fiction to make you feel the truth sometimes. Tim O'brien: "That's what fiction is for. It's for getting at the truth when the truth isn't sufficient for the truth." No one is more qualified than Kingsolver, who grew up in Appalachia and still lives there with her family, at achieving this.

A book that will make you want to hug your family and stare at the stars and ask, "What is life but a love story?"

John Donne:
"Any man's death diminishes me,
Because I am involved in mankind.
And therefore never send to know for whom the bell tolls;
It tolls for thee."

"There’s this thing that happens, let’s say at school where a bunch of guys are in the bathroom, at the urinal, laughing about some dork that made an anus of himself in gym. You’re all basically nice guys, right? You know right from wrong, and would not in a million years be brutal to the poor guy’s face. And then it happens: the dork was in the shitter. He comes out of the stall with this look. He heard everything. And you realize you’re not really that nice of a guy. This is what I would say if I could, to all smart people of the world with their dumb hillbilly jokes: We are right here in the stall. We can actually hear you."

The Things They Carried

by Tim O'Brien
Mar 09, 2026

"I can even see Timmy skating with Linda under the yellow floodlights. I'm young and happy. I'll never die. I'm skimming across the surface of my own history, moving fast, riding the melt beneath the blades, doing loops and spins, and when I take a high leap into the dark and come down thirty years later, I realize it is as Tim trying to save Timmy's life with a story."

My god, this book is so beautiful. After rereading the last chapter, I think it deserves a review.

It is simultaneously horribly sad and comforting; it makes me smile and cry. But to see it labeled as a war novel is to fundamentally misunderstand it. The war is a motif -- a motif that carries the love stories of Timmy and Linda, of O'Brien and Kiowa, of Tim and Timmy. It is funny how a story like this is truer than most non-fiction.

Along with When Breath Becomes Air, no book has shaped me more. No more beautiful a love story exists.

Update: 03/09/2026
I cry every time I finish this book. It's not a sad cry, but it's not a happy cry either. There's just something beautiful about the last chapter that I'll never be able to completely put my finger on.

In the Lake of the Woods

by Tim O'Brien
Jan 17, 2026

O'Brien is, most simply, the best writer I have ever read. No one writes as visceral. I called The Things They Carried one of the most beautiful love stories of all time, and that holds true. But In the Lake of the Woods is the most accurate love story, showcasing complexity that many try not to think about, much less write a novel on. It is utterly terrifying. I don't really want to say anything specific to the story, because I think you should just pick it up. Let Sorcerer perform his tricks and take you somewhere far away.

I was worried to read another Tim O'Brien book, as I knew nothing would probably live up to The Things They Carried. But after I couldn't get TTTC out of my head for the past few months, I decided it was time. I'm glad I did.

One of those books that I think I read at the perfect time in my life. Or maybe it is simply timeless. I'm sure this will be picked up and underlined and re-read many times.

"The ambiguity may be dissatisfying, even irritating, but this is a love story. There is no tidiness. Blame it on the human heart. One way or another, it seems, we all perform vanishing tricks, effacing history, locking up our lives and slipping day by day into the graying shadows. Our whereabouts are uncertain. All secrets lead to the dark, and beyond the dark there is only maybe."

Empire of Pain

by Patrick Radden Keefe
Jan 13, 2026

You read this book with a giant pit in your stomach. Because you know how the story ends. And yet still, every time the Sacklers manage to twist themselves out of culpability it remains just as shocking. Many of our most respected institutions -- the FDA, the Justice Department, the Trump admin, the New York Times -- all failed us. If it were fiction I'd say that it felt unrealistically evil.

But like any story, you can always look for the hope. Here, it comes in the form of the book itself. Patrick Radden Keefe, for the first time, provides a complete history of the Sacklers. The family will never face adequate consequences for the lives they destroyed, but I doubt they are happy with their own lives. And that gives me a little happiness.

"In a soft, unflinching tone, Jeff recounted the next decade of his life: he kept abusing painkillers, met a woman, fell in love, and introduced her to opioids. One day, his dealer was out of pills and said, “I’ll sell you a bag of heroin for twenty bucks.” Jeff was reluctant, but then withdrawal set in, and he acquiesced. At first, he and his girlfriend snorted heroin. “But you build up a tolerance, just like with the pills,” he said, and eventually they started injecting it. They were high when they got married. Jeff’s wife gave birth to a baby boy who was born with an opioid dependency. “The doctors weaned him off with droplets of morphine,” Jeff said."

Thinking, Fast and Slow

by Daniel Kahneman
Dec 31, 2025

"I can remember the books I have read no better than what I had for breakfast. Either way, they have shaped me."

I think (or at least I hope) that this quote will be especially true here. This book is undoubtedly dense -- I first tried picking it up in eighth grade (failed) and then again my senior year of high school (failed again) -- but holy moly the number of times I read a paragraph and just thought "wow" was absurd.

A lot of the findings Kahneman presents may already be familiar to you, but remember to keep in mind why that is.... because Kahneman COMPLETELY changed the field of psychology and behavioral economics. You previously heard this information from someone who heard it from Kahneman. Reading this book allows you to skip so many others that regurgitate his research.

It's a damn shame that many classify this as a book "for the businessman." It doesn't fit well into a genre because everyone should read it:

"It is only a slight exaggeration to say that happiness is the experience of spending time with people you love and who love you." (This, and every other statement presented, is backed up incredibly well)

The Boys of My Youth

by Jo Ann Beard
Dec 26, 2025

There was a page where I thought that Brad, Jo Ann's younger brother, was her father. And in a way, the children did raise their dad. Their alcoholic, deadbeat father. There's something uniquely tragic about lost childhood.

Coming into this book, I was hoping for something like The Things They Carried. While the essays had some interesting story vs happening truth, it disappointed in that regard, although I suppose that may have been too high a bar. Some of the essays felt like duds, but The Fourth State of Matter, Family Hour, Waiting, and The Boys of My Youth are all standalone five-stars. They are a sobering portrayal of the complexities of love.

I'm not sure how many novel ideas this book gave me, but it did reinforce the year's theme: the love you put in is the love you get out. Merry Christmas!

The Great Gatsby

by F. Scott Fitzgerald
Sep 01, 2025

I don't really know how to feel about this book. I didn't find the story all that gripping, and yet after finishing the last sentence I felt an immediate urge to start from the beginning and read it all over again.

Ashamedly, I didn't really understand the meaning of the novel until the last few pages. I wish we had read this in high school, as it feels like one of those books that you can't fully comprehend without discussion.

I'll probably revisit it in a year or so, but am not sure I'll fully grasp it until my later 20s. I hope the profundity doesn't elude me forever.

Narrative and Numbers: The Value of Stories in Business

by Aswath Damodaran
Aug 14, 2025

At the risk of sounding cocky, most of what was presented in this book was pretty obvious. Of course you need a narrative, a story, when valuing a company. It should be common sense with any understanding of efficient markets that numbers alone won’t generate alpha.

I think this could work as someone’s introduction to valuation, as it does present the ideas surrounding DCFs and intrinsic value clearly and with examples. It just doesn’t do much past that. I don’t claim to be a good investor, but I’m not sure that I have become any better after reading this.

While I’m critical, I’m still giving it 3 stars because, although not groundbreaking, Damadoran is clear and correct in what he writes.

Chasing the Scream: The First and Last Days of the War on Drugs

by Johann Hari
Aug 03, 2025

My favorite book of the year thus far. Also the most disgusting, the saddest, and the most horrifying.

Johann Hari is truly in a league of his own. His ability to tell a story through the different lenses of individuals all across the globe and bring them together into one cohesive narrative is astounding. A remarkable example of how phenomenal journalism is indistinguishable from fiction.

As someone who is staunchly against the use of drugs, I am proud to be even more staunchly against the war on drugs. Like a persistent theme for my readings this year, I would say that, at its core, this book simply advocates for compassion.

All the Light We Cannot See

by Anthony Doerr
Jul 25, 2025

To be honest, I don’t have much to say about this book. The writing was fantastic — I don’t see how anyone could dispute that — yet I found myself disappointed with the story. The reader knows that the three main storylines are going to converge at the end. It’s what everything is building towards, what keeps things interesting. But when it came, it felt a bit rushed and anticlimactic.

Alternatively, I could very well just not be smart enough, intellectual enough, to understand the deeper meanings at play here. Good book, I understand why it won the Pulitzer, just not my favorite.

Candide

by Voltaire
Jul 24, 2025

This may very well be the most timeless book I have ever read.

I assumed that the core ideas of the book would still hold value, but I did not expect to find myself genuinely laughing multiple times. It's interesting that in a world where comedy changes so rapidly, a 400-year-old French philosopher's satire still holds up to time.

But back to the ideas. There was nothing incredibly revelatory in this book, but nevertheless a single page has more value than the entirety of many "self-help" books (just my opinion, of course!). The main idea is that to achieve happiness you must "cultivate your garden." I could explain what that means, but it wouldn't be worthwhile. The first 90 pages are necessary to really understand the final 5, and so no summary will carry much value. What I will say is that humanity still struggles -- probably even more so now -- with these ideas of human suffering and happiness.

This book is short. You can read it in a day or two. Give it a shot.

After the Music Stopped: The Financial Crisis, the Response, and the Work Ahead

by Alan S. Blinder
Jul 17, 2025

My biggest takeaways:

1. I really love the Fed. While they certainly made mistakes, we would have been in a far worse position in the hands of economically illiterate elected officials.

2. Government spends != taxpayers foot the bill (always). This is an important distinction. TARP spent $700 billion, backlash ensued at perceived unfairness in regard to bank bailouts, and many still have a negative opinion of the program. Yet, in the end, the government turned a profit! They spent $700 billion (perhaps “invested” is a better term) to bolster the economy, and taxpayers weren’t left to shoulder a single cent. Someone should write a book on this concept.

I will admit that reading this book opened my eyes to just how little I actually knew about the events of the ‘08 crisis (in my defense, I was 3 years old at the time!). It was an excellent account that remained comprehensible enough to the general public while still explaining the technicals needed for a deeper understanding of the crisis. Some parts were certainly a little dense, but without ASB's engaging and energetic writing it would have been much more so.

This book is worth a read if for no other reason than to be reminded that the hubris that has plagued financial markets in the past will certainly do so again in the future. What goes up must come down.

Troubled: A Memoir of Foster Care, Family, and Social Class

by Rob Henderson
Jul 05, 2025

“A solid, two-parent home is critical for a child’s future. There is simply no shortcut.”

That’s just about the biggest takeaway from Henderson’s memoir. Being poor growing up is not a good indicator of adult happiness. Nor is having college-educated parents, or any of the typical signs of success. The most important factor — a nuclear family structure — should not be up for debate. As Henderson argues, we shouldn’t normalize single parents. This is just one of the many ‘luxury beliefs’ held by many middle-to-upper class individuals who concoct a list of rules for thee, but not for me.

Anyone who hopes to be a parent someday should read this book:

“‘Should we be reading to him more,’ [a parent] asked me.

I thought of how lonely I felt trying to teach myself how to read as a foster kid.

‘Yeah,’ I replied. ‘But not because it will expand his vocabulary. Read to him because it will remind him that you love him.’”

To Kill a Mockingbird

by Harper Lee
Jun 28, 2025

A beautiful snapshot of a young girl’s 1950s, Deep South summer. To lump this with other coming of age novels is to do it a disservice. It is but a fleeting point in time, captured by Harper Lee with confidence in its finitude.

The last couple pages are some of my favorite in any book. They so perfectly summarize the teachings of the novel, while still remaining believable from Scout's 9-year-old perspective. If you last read this for school, it’s time to pick it up again.

The Round House

by Louise Erdrich
Jun 22, 2025

Although I only spent a week on a Native American reservation, of books I have read this best encapsulates what I saw and felt. Injustice, defeat, brokenness, but also hope. You see the hope most prevalently in the kids. You see it before it is beaten out of them.

Although slow at times, read this novel to better understand our world. But also read this novel to remember the consequences of your actions and what it means to be a good friend and person.

Hillbilly Elegy: A Memoir of a Family and Culture in Crisis

by J.D. Vance
Jun 15, 2025

It's a shame that many seem incapable of separating this work of art from their opinions of the author behind it. I don't like JD Vance, I don't agree with many of his politics, but I can still recognize that this book came from the heart, shedding light on a topic and people that have largely been ignored by our country. It should be mandatory reading for all those who want to -- or claim to -- understand the division in American politics and culture.

by Robert James Waller
Jun 12, 2025

At times it felt like it was trying too hard to be something deeper, when in reality it lacked the depth that I was hoping for when I picked it up. Not bad, but also not profound. The only idea I really found interesting was that of the third being in a relationship—the “us.” Considering its short length, perhaps that is enough to make this novel worth it.
